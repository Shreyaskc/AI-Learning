Chapter 4: Optimization: how learning changes parameters. An objective connects data to parameters.

Call all adjustable model numbers theta. Let L measure one prediction error, and J the average training error. For examples (x sub i,y sub i), empirical risk is that average loss The objective J of theta is the mean, over n examples, of the loss between the model prediction for each input and its target. It measures fit on this sample. Population risk is the expectation over future examples; minimizing sample loss does not guarantee minimizing that expectation. A penalty can prefer some solutions even when their sample fit is similar. Its strength is lambda; w is the vector of penalized weights. Adding this regularizer gives: The regularized objective is J plus lambda divided by two times the squared Euclidean norm of the weights. L2 penalizes large coefficients; it is a modeling choice related to a preference for smoother or smaller-coefficient solutions, not universal protection against leakage.

Gradient descent uses the gradient as a direction and a positive step size eta, called the learning rate: The new weight equals the old weight minus learning rate eta times the gradient of objective J. With eta equals zero point one in the same one-example problem, the next update takes w from 1.6 to 1.96: residual 3.2 minus 5 equals negative 1.8, gradient negative 3.6, update plus zero point three six. Repeating this calculation is training; choosing an oversized step can undo progress.

For many examples, average the per-example gradients. Full-batch descent uses the entire training set for each update; stochastic gradient descent uses one or a small batch. A minibatch trades noisy estimates for cheaper and more frequent updates. Shuffle only in ways compatible with the data and model; sequence labels and time-based evaluation still require care. An epoch is a pass through the training examples, not a universal unit of compute across datasets.
