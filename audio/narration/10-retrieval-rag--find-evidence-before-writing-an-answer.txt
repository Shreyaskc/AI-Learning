Chapter 10: Retrieval, ranking and grounded generation. Find evidence before writing an answer.

A search system maps a query to candidate documents. An inverted index maps terms to postings containing document identifiers and possibly positions. B M twenty five scores query-term matches using term rarity, saturation and document-length normalization. Dense retrieval encodes queries and documents into vectors and scores their relationships. A learned embedding can connect paraphrases absent exact overlap, but may miss exact identifiers, negation or domain distinctions. Lexical and dense approaches often complement each other; evaluate on labeled queries rather than assuming one always wins.

Exact vector search compares every candidate. Approximate nearest-neighbor (approximate nearest neighbor) structures reduce search work by accepting some missed neighbors. Graph-based indexes such as HNSW navigate neighbor links; quantization can compress vectors. Index settings trade memory, build and update cost, latency and recall. approximate nearest neighbor recall relative to exact neighbors is distinct from relevance recall against human labels. A perfect approximation to a poor embedding still retrieves poorly.

Candidate generation favors recall; a reranker spends more work on a smaller set. A bi-encoder embeds query and document independently, enabling stored vectors. A cross-encoder jointly processes both and can inspect detailed interactions, usually at higher per-pair cost. Hybrid retrieval can combine lexical and dense candidate lists with reciprocal rank fusion: score(d) equals sum sub lists 1 divided by (k plus rank sub list(d)). Here k is a smoothing constant, not the number of results; rank starts at 1. Scores are ranking tools, not relevance probabilities.
