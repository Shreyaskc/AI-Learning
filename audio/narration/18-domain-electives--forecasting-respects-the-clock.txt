Chapter 18: Domain electives: time, recommendations and anomalies. Forecasting respects the clock.

Imagine making a forecast on Monday for Tuesday’s load. Monday is the forecast origin; the one-day distance to Tuesday is the horizon. Forecasting predicts a value at that horizon using only information available at the origin. Seasonality repeats patterns; trend is a systematic direction; residuals are what remains under a chosen model. A strong baseline can be “last observed value” or “same seasonal position last week.” Compare using rolling-origin evaluation: train up to t, predict t plus h, advance origin, and refit only as declared. Do not let future normalization statistics or revised external indicators enter historical forecasts.

Worked example: daily equipment loads are [10,12,11,13]. A one-step last-value forecast for the fourth day is 11, absolute error 2. Over eligible origins forecasting values [12,11,13] from [10,12,11], absolute errors are [2,1,2], MAE equals 5 divided by 3 approximately 1.667. A model must beat this baseline under the same origins and data availability. With uncertainty intervals, report coverage by horizon, not only pooled error.

Time series often have dependence, nonstationarity and overlapping targets. Randomly splitting overlapping windows can leak almost identical histories across train and test. A gap between splits may be needed where labels and windows overlap. Exogenous predictors are valid only if actually known at forecast time. A revised annual statistic is not the same feature as the initially published value.
