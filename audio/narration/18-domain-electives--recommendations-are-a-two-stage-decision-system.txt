Chapter 18: Domain electives: time, recommendations and anomalies. Recommendations are a two-stage decision system.

Candidate generation chooses a manageable set; ranking orders it for a user and context. Popularity is a meaningful baseline. Collaborative filtering exploits patterns of user-item interaction; content models use item and user attributes. Matrix factorization approximates a user-item score with the dot product of u and v, a learned low-dimensional representation. Implicit feedback such as clicks mixes preference with exposure: an unclicked item may never have been shown.

Worked ranking: a user has two held-out relevant items A and C. Top three recommendations [B,A,C] have precision@3 equals 2 divided by 3, recall@3 equals 1, reciprocal rank equals 1 divided by 2. normalized discounted cumulative gain gives earlier relevant results more weight and divides by the best possible ordering. For binary relevance, a common discounted gain is 1 divided by log2(rank plus 1) at a relevant position, with rank starting at 1. Comparing the sum with its ideal-order value produces the normalized score; graded labels can give different gains. This is another ranking objective, not a direct user-satisfaction measurement. Offline ranking improvements may reflect popularity or position bias rather than better user outcomes. Evaluate cold-start users and items, diversity, novelty and long-term behavior under a clear product objective.

The next ideas are an optional deeper pass on feedback. A contextual bandit chooses an action with a reward and learns from partial feedback. It trades exploration and exploitation; unlike a full reinforcement-learning formulation, the basic model does not optimize a long sequence of state transitions. Logged-policy evaluation requires support and overlap: you cannot reliably estimate actions that had essentially no chance of being taken without additional assumptions. Propensity weighting can have high variance when propensities are tiny.
